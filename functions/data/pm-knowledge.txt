## How property management companies work (vetted brief)
A third-party PMC is hired by the owner (or the GP of a fund) to run the asset. The PMC does not usually own the building. The scoreboard is NOI — net operating income — because that number drives valuations, loan covenants, and whether the management agreement gets renewed.
The org chart on a typical community: Community / property manager, assistant, leasing consultants, and a maintenance tech or two. Regional managers cover 4–12 sites. Corporate has accounting, compliance, and owner reporting. Midsize Oregon shops often run dual roles — the same person is leasing and taking work orders after 4pm.
How the PMC gets paid: Management fee as a percent of collected rent (often 3–8% depending on asset and market), plus leasing commissions, renewal fees, markups on maintenance, and admin fees. If occupancy or collections slip, the PMC’s fee slips. That is why vacancy days, delinquencies, and avoidable truck rolls are not “ops trivia” — they are the business model.
The daily work that eats the day: Resident texts and calls (hours, packages, parking, rent, noise). Maintenance intake and vendor dispatch. ILS/website leads, tours, applications, screening. Delinquency outreach. Owner packets. Inspections and turns. Industry surveys routinely put tenant communications near 40% of a manager’s time, with maintenance as the largest slice.
Why phones still fail: Multifamily lines miss a large share of inbound calls — published estimates often land around 50–60%, and many of those callers never try again. A missed leasing call can be a full vacancy month. A delayed leak can turn a $1,200 plumber into an $8,000 unit-below claim. Answering services take messages; they rarely triage, write a work order, or book a tour.
What “good” looks like to an owner: Occupancy and collections, expense control vs budget, clean turns, low skip/eviction, and a report they can forward to their lender without a follow-up call. AI that cannot show up as a line item on that report does not survive the next budget meeting.

### HVAC
Cooling, heating, airflow, and controls. Diagnose on the truck; ManyDoors triages the resident call first.
First questions: Is it no heat, no cooling, or weak airflow? Does the indoor fan run? Any unusual noise or burning smell? Have they checked the thermostat mode, setpoint, and batteries? Filter last changed? Outdoor unit blocked by debris?
Self-help: Thermostat set to HEAT or COOL (not OFF/FAN), setpoint past room temp, fresh batteries. Replace a clogged filter — the most common “AC not cooling” ticket in multifamily. Clear 2 ft around the outdoor condenser; straighten bent fins only if safe. If the fan runs but no temperature change, dispatch HVAC — likely refrigerant, capacitor, or control board.
Escalate: Gas smell, carbon monoxide alarm, burning smell, or smoke from the unit. No heat in freezing weather (life-safety in many jurisdictions). Water pouring from the air handler (overflow pan / condensate clog can damage units below).

### Plumbing
Drains, water heaters, fixtures, and leaks. Stop the water first, then decide self-help vs truck roll.
First questions: Is water actively leaking, or is it a slow drip / clog? Which fixture — toilet, sink, tub, water heater, or in-wall? Can they shut the fixture stop or unit shutoff? Any sewage smell or water on the floor of a unit below?
Self-help: Running toilet: jiggle the handle; if it keeps filling, lift the tank lid and seat the flapper — often stops a 24/7 water waste ticket. Garbage disposal humming: power OFF, then press the red RESET on the underside; hex key in the bottom center to free a jam. Slow drain: plunger first. Do not mix drain chemicals if a plumber may snake the line.
Escalate: Burst pipe, flooding, or water entering electrical areas. Sewage backup. No water to the building or a suspected main-line break. Gas water heater with gas odor.

### Electrical
Outlets, breakers, lighting, and GFCI. Residents never open panels beyond a labeled breaker reset.
First questions: One outlet, one room, or the whole unit? Any GFCI buttons (TEST/RESET) in kitchen, bath, garage, or patio? Did a breaker trip? Any burning smell, scorch marks, or buzzing?
Self-help: Dead kitchen/bath/garage outlet: find a nearby GFCI and press RESET. Check the breaker panel for a tripped breaker (center position — fully OFF then ON). Whole-unit outage: confirm neighbors still have power before dispatching; may be utility.
Escalate: Sparks, smoke, burning smell, scorched outlets, or buzzing in the panel. Water + electricity. Repeated breaker trips after a reset — do not keep resetting.

### Pool Service
Pumps, chemistry, heaters, and equipment. ManyDoors answers amenity hours from the property profile; Pros handles equipment.
First questions: Is this hours/rules, cloudy water, a pump that will not prime, or a heater that will not fire? Any alarm on the equipment pad? Is the pump running?
Self-help: Hours and guest rules come from the property knowledge base — do not invent closing times. Cloudy water: do not tell residents to dump chemicals. Log it for the pool tech (filter, sanitizer, CYA). Pump running dry or loud grinding: shut it off and dispatch — do not run a dry pump.
Escalate: Green/cloudy water with scheduled parties (health risk). Gas heater odor. Electrical faults at the pad.

### Property Maintenance
Appliances, unit turns, locks, and punch lists. This is the default queue when the trade is unclear.
First questions: Which unit and which room? Appliance make/model if visible? Can the resident stay in the unit safely?
Self-help: Fridge not cold: confirm it is plugged in, condenser coils not packed with dust, and door seals close. Washer will not drain: check the coin trap / drain hose kink before a full appliance call. Minor cosmetic items (drip, scuff, slow closer) can wait for a bundled make-ready unless they create a trip/slip or lockout.
Escalate: Lockout, failed lock, or break-in. No heat, flood, fire, gas, or sewage — reclassify to the emergency trade.

### Fiber & Low Voltage
Structured cabling, access control, cameras, and ISP drops. Do not guess ISP outages as in-unit wiring.
First questions: Is it one unit, one closet, or the whole building? Did the ISP already confirm their side is up? Any recent construction, unit turn, or access-control change?
Self-help: Power-cycle ONT/router 60 seconds. Confirm other devices on Wi-Fi vs Ethernet. Building-wide outage: check the MDF/IDF first; do not send six techs to six units.
Escalate: Access control failure that locks residents out of the building. Life-safety cameras/intercoms down at a controlled entry.
